Presentation Guide: AttackAware PolyIoM
The core ideas behind each of the 24 slides, explained simply


HOW TO USE THIS GUIDE
=====================

This guide does not repeat what is written on the slides. For each slide it
explains the idea underneath: what the concept is, how it works, and why it
matters. Read it once before presenting, so that you can explain any slide in
your own words and answer questions about it.


PART 1: THE QUESTION
====================


Slide 1: Title
--------------

Core idea: a protection scheme is only useful if a stolen template can be
cancelled, and this paper finds which part of the scheme makes cancelling
possible.

Cancelable biometrics. A password leak is a nuisance: you choose a new password.
A face or voice leak is permanent, because you have only one face and one voice.
Cancelable biometrics solve this by never storing the real biometric. Instead,
the system stores a version transformed by a secret key. If that version leaks,
the system picks a new key and builds a new version from the same face. The two
versions look unrelated, so the leaked one becomes worthless. It works like a
bank card: the card number can be cancelled and reissued, while your bank
account stays the same.

Keyed compression. "Compression" means turning a long list of numbers into a
shorter one. "Keyed" means the way it is shortened depends on a secret key. The
title claims that this step, and not the hashing that follows it, is what makes
cancelling work. Slide 4 explains why.

Pre-registration. Researchers can fool themselves without meaning to. If you try
many settings and then report the one that looks best, the result looks better
than it truly is. Pre-registration prevents this. You write down the plan, the
settings and the pass marks before you look at the test data, and you stick to
them. It is like a teacher fixing the pass mark before marking the exams, rather
than after seeing the scores.

In one line: the paper asks which part of the protection actually lets us cancel
a stolen template, and tests it with rules fixed in advance.


Slide 2: Abstract
-----------------

Core idea: a protection scheme must be judged on all its requirements at the
same time, at one fixed setting.

Why one setting matters. Every biometric system has a dial, the decision
threshold, that sets how strict it is. Turn it up and strangers are rarely
accepted, but real users are often rejected. Turn it down and the opposite
happens. Each requirement can be made to look good at some position of the dial.
Hashing can also be made more or less detailed. A fair evaluation picks one
position, locks it, and reports every requirement there. That is what "one
sealed operating point" means.

The trade-off. The abstract ends with the paper's main message: there is no free
lunch. The polynomial gives one benefit (it hides the real biometric) and costs
something in return (accuracy, and less even cancelling). A good paper states
this openly instead of claiming the method is better at everything.

In one line: the system meets all four requirements at one locked setting, and
each benefit has a measured cost.


Slide 3: The problem
--------------------

Core idea: the international standard ISO/IEC 24745 asks four things of a
protected template, and each one protects against a different harm.

Recognition performance protects usefulness. Protection always throws some
information away, and less information means more recognition mistakes. A scheme
that is secure but cannot recognise people is useless.

Irreversibility protects the biometric itself. If a thief can turn a stolen
template back into a face or voice, they could use it to impersonate the person
on other systems, or simply learn what the person looks or sounds like.

Unlinkability protects privacy. Suppose a bank and a hospital both store your
protected face with different keys. If someone can tell that the two templates
belong to the same person, they can track you across services. Unlinkability
means they cannot.

Revocability protects the future. After a leak, the system must be able to issue
a new template and make the old one useless.

Why the fourth is rarely tested. The first three can be measured on templates as
they are, at one moment. Revocability is a story with several steps: steal a
template, attack it, change the key, then try the attack again. Because it needs
this sequence, many studies assume it holds instead of testing it.

In one line: four requirements, four different harms, and the one about
cancelling is the one most often left untested.


Slide 4: The scheme and the three questions
-------------------------------------------

Core idea: what an attacker can recover from a stolen template decides whether
changing the key helps.

The embedding. A face or voice model turns a photo or recording into a list of
numbers called an embedding. You can picture it as a point in space. Recordings
of the same person land close together, and different people land far apart. The
system decides "same person" by measuring how close two points are.

Why hashing alone cannot be cancelled. IoM hashing compares many random
combinations of the embedding and records which one is largest in each group.
Each record is a small clue about the direction of the embedding. With hundreds
of clues, an attacker can work backwards and rebuild the embedding itself almost
exactly. That embedding is the person's real voice, and it does not depend on
any key. So after the key changes, the attacker simply runs the new system on
the rebuilt voice and gets in again. Changing the key is like changing the lock
on a door when the thief has a copy of your actual fingerprint: the fingerprint
still opens the new lock.

Why adding keyed compression fixes it. If the embedding is first shrunk and
mixed by a secret key, the stored template only describes this mixed version.
The attacker can now rebuild only the mixed version, which belongs to the old
key. When the key changes, the mixing changes too, and the attacker's rebuilt
version no longer fits. Now the thief has a copy of an old key-shaped
impression, not of your fingerprint, and changing the lock helps.

What the polynomial adds. Both a simple linear mixing and the polynomial give
this cancelling benefit. The polynomial adds something more: it is harder to
work backwards through, so the attacker learns less about the real voice. This
costs some accuracy.

In one line: cancelling works only if the attacker cannot recover the real
biometric; keyed compression makes sure of that, and the polynomial hides it
even better.


Slide 5: Contributions
----------------------

Core idea: the main contribution is a fairer way of testing, not only a new
scheme.

Ablation. To find out what each part of a recipe does, you cook it again with
one ingredient left out and taste the difference. An ablation does the same with
a system: remove one step, keep everything else the same, and measure what
changes. Without it, a multi-step system gets credit for its whole design, even
if one step does all the work.

Attacking the baselines. If you attack only your own method, you cannot tell
whether a result comes from your method or would happen to any method. Attacking
the simple versions with the same attack gives a fair comparison.

Reporting against the unprotected system. The honest question is how much
protection costs. That can only be answered by comparing with the same system
without protection, on the same people.

Confirmatory versus exploratory. Results planned in advance and run once are
confirmatory, which is strong evidence. Analyses added later, after seeing some
data, are exploratory or secondary, which is supporting evidence. Keeping the
two apart tells the reader how much weight each result can carry.

In one line: we test each part separately, attack everything equally, and say
clearly which results were planned and which were added later.


Slide 6: Background
-------------------

Core idea: earlier methods and earlier ways of testing them both have known weak
spots.

Random projection and BioHashing. An early idea was to multiply the embedding by
a secret random matrix, which is a linear transformation. This is easy and keeps
recognition good. But a linear transformation can be undone by solving
equations. If the key leaks along with the template, much of the original can be
recovered.

IoM hashing. Index-of-Maximum hashing keeps only the position of the winner in
each group, not the values. Many different inputs give the same output, so the
output does not directly reveal the input. That is its strength. As Slide 4
shows, though, many clues together can still reveal a lot.

Three habits that weaken many studies:
- Choosing the threshold on the test data. This gives an over-optimistic result,
  like setting the pass mark after seeing the scores.
- No ablation. A two-step method is tested as a whole, so nobody knows which
  step matters.
- Irreversibility claimed, not tested. The paper argues the template cannot be
  reversed instead of trying to reverse it with a real attack.

In one line: we avoid the three common habits and add the missing cancelling
test.


PART 2: HOW WE DID IT
=====================


Slide 7: The face and voice models
----------------------------------

Core idea: freeze the recognition models so that every change in results comes
from the protection alone.

Pretrained models as feature extractors. Building a good face or voice model
needs millions of examples. Instead, we use models others have already trained,
FaceNet for faces and ECAPA-TDNN for voices. They turn input into an embedding,
the list of numbers that captures who the person is.

Why frozen. "Frozen" means the models are used exactly as downloaded and never
retrained on our data. If we retrained them, an improvement could come from the
better model rather than the protection. Freezing isolates the one thing we want
to study. It also stops the model from accidentally learning from the test
people.

Normalisation. Each embedding is scaled to the same length. Only its direction
matters, so two embeddings are compared by the angle between them. This angle-
based measure is called cosine similarity: 1 means pointing the same way, 0
means unrelated.

In one line: we used standard models unchanged so that the experiment measures
only the protection.


Slide 8: How a template is made and checked
-------------------------------------------

Core idea: each step throws away information on purpose, which protects the
person but costs some accuracy.

Hardening with overlapping windows. The polynomial looks at a few neighbouring
numbers at a time, mixes them into one, then slides along with some overlap. The
overlap means each number influences more than one output, so no single output
depends on just one input. Shrinking 192 numbers to 48 loses information. That
loss is part of the protection, because many different voices now produce the
same 48 numbers.

Non-linear mixing. A polynomial uses squares and other powers, not only
multiplication by fixed numbers. Linear mixing is like a recipe you can reverse
with equations. Non-linear mixing is more like baking: many different ingredient
amounts can give a cake that looks the same, so you cannot read the exact recipe
back from the cake.

Index-of-Maximum hashing. For each group, the system creates several random
combinations and records only which one won. Small noise rarely changes the
winner, so two recordings of the same person tend to give the same positions.
The actual values are never stored.

Matching by counting agreement. To check a person, the system counts how many
group winners agree between the stored template and the new one. If enough
agree, the person is accepted. The threshold is the trade-off dial from Slide 2.

In one line: mix, shrink, keep only the winners, then count matching winners;
each step hides information and costs a little accuracy.


Slide 9: Choosing the key and the settings
------------------------------------------

Core idea: choosing and testing must use different data, and choosing a key is
not the same as proving it is safe.

Selecting on separate data. When you try many options and keep the best one,
part of its good score is luck. If you then report that same score, you
overstate how good it is. This is sometimes called the winner's curse. The cure
is to choose on one group of people and measure on another group the choice has
never seen.

Two-stage selection. Stage A chooses the secret polynomial key. Stage B chooses
how detailed the hashing is: how many groups, how many options per group, and
how much the windows overlap. Each stage uses its own group of people, so
neither choice is tuned to the final test.

The selection rule. Among settings that recognise people well enough, pick the
one that is hardest to link. This puts privacy first, as long as accuracy stays
above a minimum.

Selection is not evaluation. Stage A used a quick attack to screen keys. Passing
a screening test is not proof of safety, in the same way a job interview is not
proof of good work. The real attack test comes later, on Slide 17.

In one line: choose on one group, test on another, and do not mistake a
screening test for real proof.


Slide 10: Splitting the people into groups
------------------------------------------

Core idea: the test must use people the system has never seen, and decisions
must be locked before testing.

Identity-disjoint splits. The groups are split by person, not by photo or
recording. If the same person appeared in both the tuning and the test groups,
the system would partly be recognising someone it already knew. Splitting by
person tests what really matters: how the system handles new users.

External validation. Testing on a completely different recording collection
checks whether the system still works under different conditions: different
microphones, accents and rooms. A method that works on only one collection may
have adapted to that collection's quirks.

Sealing. Sealing means writing the key, settings and threshold into a locked
record before the test data are opened, with checks that nothing changed
afterwards. It turns "we did not change anything" from a promise into something
that can be verified.

In one line: new people for testing, a second collection for a reality check,
and locked decisions so no one can tune after seeing the results.


Slide 11: How we measured
-------------------------

Core idea: each measure answers a different question, and every result comes
with an honest range of uncertainty.

EER, TAR and FMR. A system can make two kinds of mistake: rejecting a real user
(false reject) or accepting a stranger (false match). Moving the threshold
trades one for the other. EER is the error rate at the point where both kinds
are equal, so it summarises accuracy without choosing a threshold. TAR and FMR
are measured at the actual locked threshold, which is what a real user would
experience.

Linkability (Dsys). Take two protected templates made with different keys.
Compare their scores when they come from the same person with their scores when
they come from different people. If these two groups of scores look the same, an
observer cannot tell which pairs are the same person, so the templates are
unlinkable. Dsys measures how far apart the two groups are, from 0 (identical,
unlinkable) to 1 (completely separate, fully linkable).

Attack success and post-revocation acceptance. These two measures (SAR and PRAR)
are explained on Slides 17 and 18.

Bootstrap intervals. We have only one sample of people, so every result has some
luck in it. The bootstrap estimates how much. It builds many new samples by
drawing people at random from the ones we have (some twice, some not at all),
recalculates the result each time, and looks at how much it moves. The middle
95% of those results gives the interval.

Why resample people, not comparisons. One person produces many comparisons, and
they are not independent: a person with a quiet voice affects all of their
comparisons in the same way. Treating thousands of comparisons as independent
would make us look far more certain than we are. Resampling whole people keeps
the uncertainty honest. For the cancelling test, results also vary a lot from
key to key, so keys are resampled too.

In one line: different measures for different requirements, and uncertainty
estimated by resampling whatever really varies.


Slide 12: Tools and data
------------------------

Core idea: a result is only trustworthy if someone else can repeat it exactly.

Version pinning. Software changes over time, and a newer version can give
slightly different numbers. Recording the exact version of every tool means
anyone can rebuild the same setup.

Fixed random seeds. Random choices, such as how people are split or which keys
are drawn, are made from one fixed starting number (the seed). The same seed
always gives the same random choices, so the whole study can be re-run and give
identical results.

Keeping future tests clean. A later face test is planned on another collection
called CFP. If the same people appeared in both collections, that future test
would not really use new people. So 100 people whose names also appear in CFP
were removed now, in advance.

In one line: exact versions and fixed seeds make the results repeatable, and
removing overlapping people keeps future tests fair.


PART 3: RESULTS
===============


Slide 13: The locked settings
-----------------------------

Core idea: a protection scheme cannot do better than the recognition model
underneath it, and targets must be set before testing.

The encoder ceiling. Protection works on the embedding the model produces. If
the model itself confuses two people, no protection can separate them again. So
the model's own error rate is roughly the best the protected system can hope
for. The face model makes more mistakes than the voice model on these photos,
which is why face needed an easier target.

Relaxing a target honestly. Changing a target is not cheating if it is done
before looking at the test data, for a stated reason, and reported openly. It
becomes cheating only when targets are moved after seeing the results. Here the
face target was relaxed during development, when it became clear no setting
could reach it, and then locked.

In one line: protection inherits the limits of the underlying model, and
changing a target is fair only when it happens before testing.


Slide 14: Testing on new people
-------------------------------

Core idea: the honest measure of protection is its cost compared with the
unprotected system, and that cost must be stated with its uncertainty.

Development versus test. Results on the people used for tuning are usually
better than on new people, because the settings were chosen to suit the tuning
group. The test on new people shows the realistic performance.

Cost of protection. The fair question is not "is the error low?" but "how much
higher is the error because of the protection?" The unprotected system is run on
the same people, and the difference is the cost. On voice the cost is clear and
firm. On face the uncertainty is too wide to measure the cost reliably, so the
paper says so instead of guessing.

Why no ratio. The unprotected voice system makes almost no mistakes, close to
zero. Saying protection is "20 times worse" would be misleading, because
dividing by a number near zero gives a huge ratio from a tiny absolute
difference. The paper reports the difference in percentage points instead.

Upper bounds when there are no errors. If a system makes zero or almost zero
mistakes in a test, that does not prove its true error rate is zero. It means
the rate is below some small limit. Statistics gives that limit (an upper
bound), and the paper reports the bound rather than a misleading exact number.

In one line: report protection as a cost against the unprotected system, avoid
misleading ratios, and admit when the data cannot settle a question.


Slide 15: Testing on a different voice collection
-------------------------------------------------

Core idea: a system tuned on one collection may behave differently on another,
and a fixed threshold is the first thing to drift.

Domain shift. Different collections differ in microphones, accents, rooms and
speaking style. These differences move all the similarity scores a little, which
is called domain shift. A system that has quietly adapted to one collection can
lose accuracy on another.

Why overall accuracy held but false matches rose. The overall error rate (EER)
is measured at the best threshold for the new data, so it adapts automatically.
The false match rate is measured at the locked threshold, which was set on the
first collection. If impostor scores shift slightly upward on the new
collection, more of them cross the old threshold. So the error rate can stay
similar while the false match rate goes above target. In practice, a deployed
system would recalibrate its threshold for a new setting.

In one line: the protection kept working on new data; only the fixed threshold
needed recalibrating, as expected under domain shift.


Slide 16: What the polynomial costs
-----------------------------------

Core idea: an ablation splits the total cost into parts, so each step is held
responsible for its own share.

Three versions, one difference at a time. Hashing alone is the starting point.
Adding a simple linear shrinking step shows the cost of making the data smaller.
Replacing the linear step with the polynomial shows the extra cost of non-linear
mixing. Because only one thing changes at each step, each difference can be
blamed on that one change.

Why shrinking costs accuracy. Fewer numbers carry less detail about the person,
so two similar people are confused more often.

Why non-linear mixing costs more. Non-linear mixing can bend small differences
between recordings of the same person into larger differences after mixing. So
two recordings of the same person agree a little less after the polynomial.

Paired comparison. All three versions are tested on exactly the same people and
recordings. Differences between people therefore cancel out, and the comparison
is sharper than comparing different groups.

In one line: most of the accuracy cost comes from the polynomial's non-linear
mixing, not from the shrinking or the hashing.


Slide 17: What an attacker can and cannot do
--------------------------------------------

Core idea: getting accepted and recovering the real biometric are two different
things, and a scheme can prevent one without preventing the other.

The strongest attacker. Security researchers assume the attacker knows
everything except the secret they are trying to find. This is Kerckhoffs's
principle. Here we go further and give the attacker the keys too. This worst
case shows what protection remains once everything else has leaked.

How the attack works. The attacker starts from a random guess and nudges it,
step by step, until the system produces the stolen template from it. There is
one difficulty: "which one is largest" gives no sense of direction for how to
improve the guess. The attack therefore uses a soft version of "largest", one
that blends the options and gives a direction. It then gradually sharpens it
back into a hard choice. This sharpening is called annealing.

Acceptance versus recovery. The system only checks whether the template matches.
Any input that produces the right template is accepted, whether or not it is the
real person. Because the polynomial and the hashing throw information away, many
different inputs produce the same template. The attacker finds one of them, and
that is enough to get in. So the attack succeeds against every version.

Why the polynomial still hides the person. Finding an input that gets in is not
the same as finding the real input. A linear shrinking step keeps a fixed part
of the information unchanged, so any input that fits must agree with the real
voice on that part, and the attacker's copy partly resembles the real voice. The
polynomial mixes non-linearly, so the inputs that fit can be very different from
the real voice, and the attacker's copy is close to a random guess. In plain
words: knowing that two numbers add up to 10 does not tell you they were 3 and
7.

Why this makes cancelling essential. Since a well-informed attacker can always
get in with a stolen template, the real defence is to cancel the template as
soon as a theft is known. That is tested on the next slide.

In one line: the attacker can forge a match but cannot learn the real voice, so
the defence that matters is cancelling the stolen template.


Slide 18: Can a stolen template be cancelled?
---------------------------------------------

Core idea: cancelling works only if what the attacker recovers depends on the
old key.

The cancelling test, step by step:
1. Steal a template.
2. Build a fake input from it, as on Slide 17.
3. Change the keys and re-enrol the person.
4. Try the old fake against the new template.
If cancelling works, the old fake should now fail.

Why hashing alone fails every time. Without keyed compression, the fake input is
essentially the real voice, because the attack recovers the embedding itself.
The real voice matches the person under any key, so the old fake keeps working
forever.

Why keyed compression succeeds. With keyed compression, the fake input only fits
the old mixing. Under a new key the mixing is different, and the old fake no
longer produces a matching template.

Key-to-key variation. Most new keys cancel perfectly, but a few cancel poorly,
and the paper does not yet know why. This is why the average can mislead: a few
bad keys raise the average even when the typical key is perfect. It is also why
the uncertainty must account for keys, not just people.

The correction. An early test used only 3 keys and suggested the polynomial
cancelled worse than the linear map. With 3 keys, one unusual key can swing the
result, and resampling only people hides that. With 40 keys and resampling of
keys as well, the difference disappeared. The paper withdrew the claim. This
shows the lesson of Slide 11 in action: resample whatever really varies, or you
will be over-confident.

In one line: keyed compression makes stolen templates cancellable because the
attacker's copy is tied to the old key, and testing with too few keys can give
false conclusions.


Slide 19: Summary of results
----------------------------

Core idea: each result is labelled by how strong its evidence is.

Confirmatory results were in the plan written before testing and were run once,
so they cannot have been shaped by the data. They carry the most weight.
Secondary results were added later to explain the confirmatory ones. They are
informative, but readers should give them somewhat less weight, because the
decision to run them was made after seeing some data.

The overall picture. Keyed compression provides cancelling, and the polynomial
provides concealment. These are two different jobs done by two different
properties of the design, which is the paper's central message.

In one line: the four requirements in one view, with an honest label for how
strong each piece of evidence is.


PART 4: WHAT IT MEANS
=====================


Slide 20: Discussion
--------------------

Core idea: what you measure decides what you conclude, so a study must measure
the right things.

Fidelity is not safety. A study that only checks how well the real voice can be
rebuilt would call the polynomial very safe. But the attacker can still get in.
Measuring only one notion of irreversibility gives an incomplete and over-
optimistic picture.

The measure that separates designs. On accuracy the versions differ a little, on
linkability hardly at all, and against a fully informed attacker they all fail
to block entry. Only the cancelling test shows a dramatic difference: hashing
alone never cancels, while keyed compression does. So the least-tested
requirement turns out to be the most informative one.

Advice for the field. Attack the simple versions too, so that differences mean
something. Test each step separately. When results depend on random keys, test
many keys, not a few.

In one line: the requirement that most studies skip is the one that best reveals
whether a design works.


Slide 21: Which design to choose
--------------------------------

Core idea: the right design depends on which harm you most need to prevent.

Two kinds of harm. If a template leaks, two bad things can follow. First, the
thief can log in as the person. This harm can be repaired, because cancelling
the template and issuing a new key stops it. Second, the thief can learn the
person's real face or voice. This harm cannot be repaired, because nobody can be
given a new face.

Matching the design to the harm. The polynomial is better at preventing the harm
that cannot be repaired, since it hides the real biometric. It pays for this
with lower accuracy and less even cancelling. The linear map is more accurate
and cancels more reliably, but reveals more of the biometric. A system for
highly sensitive uses might prefer the polynomial. A system where convenience
and dependable cancelling matter most might prefer the linear map.

The rule that holds either way. Always use keyed compression before hashing.
Without it, a stolen template can never be cancelled, whichever other choices
are made.

In one line: choose the polynomial to protect the irreplaceable biometric, the
linear map for accuracy and reliable cancelling, and never skip keyed
compression.


Slide 22: Limitations
---------------------

Core idea: limitations tell the audience how far each result can be trusted and
where it might not apply.

Statistical power. With a modest number of test people, small differences cannot
be told apart from luck. That is why some face results are described but not
claimed as firm.

Generalisation. Results checked on one collection may not hold on another. Voice
was checked on a second collection, face was not, so face results are less
certain to transfer.

One attack is a lower bound. If one attack recovers a certain amount, a cleverer
attack might recover more. So the attack results show the minimum risk, not the
maximum.

Open questions. Some keys cancel poorly for reasons not yet understood. The
linear map was not tuned on its own, so it might do even better with its own
settings.

In one line: these limits mark where the evidence is strong and where more work
is needed; none of them reverses the main findings.


Slide 23: Conclusion
--------------------

Core idea: two parts of the design do two different jobs, and the choice between
them is a trade-off.

The message to remember. Keyed compression is what lets a stolen template be
cancelled. The polynomial is what hides the real biometric from an attacker.
They are separate benefits, and the second costs accuracy.

The message for researchers. To evaluate such schemes fairly, attack the simple
versions with the same attack, test each step on its own, compare with the
unprotected system, and test what happens after a template is cancelled.

Verifiability. Every number can be re-derived from the stored results with one
command. Two independent implementations produce identical templates, which
shows the results do not depend on a hidden detail of one program.

In one line: compress under a key to make cancelling possible, add the
polynomial when hiding the biometric is worth some accuracy, and test schemes
the way this paper does.


Slide 24: Declarations
----------------------

Core idea: openness about methods, data and ethics is part of trustworthy
research.

AI assistance. The paper states exactly what the AI helped with and confirms
that the core design and main results came before that help and were not
changed. This lets readers judge the work fairly.

Why embeddings are not shared. An embedding is not anonymous. It is a
description of a person's face or voice, and Slide 17 shows it can be partly
rebuilt. Sharing it would be sharing biometric data, so only code and results
are shared.

Ethical attack research. Studying attacks is how defences improve. Doing it
responsibly means attacking only templates the researchers created themselves,
on public data, never a live system or real users.

In one line: we are open about how the work was done, careful with biometric
data, and responsible about attack experiments.


THREE IDEAS TO REMEMBER
=======================

- What an attacker recovers decides whether cancelling works. If they recover
  the real biometric, no key change helps. Keyed compression makes sure they
  recover only something tied to the old key.
- Getting in and learning who you are are different harms. The polynomial does
  not stop the first, but it greatly reduces the second.
- Protection always costs something. The polynomial trades a little accuracy for
  better hiding, and the right choice depends on which harm matters most.


QUESTIONS PEOPLE MAY ASK
========================

Q: If the attacker always gets in, what is the point of the protection?
A: The attacker in our test already knows every secret key, which is the worst
   case. Protection then works in two ways. The attacker learns little about the
   real person, and once the theft is noticed the template is cancelled, after
   which the old fake stops working for most keys.

Q: Why does changing the key not help when only hashing is used?
A: Because the attack rebuilds the person's real embedding, which does not
   depend on any key. The real biometric matches under any new key.

Q: Why is the polynomial better at hiding than a linear map?
A: A linear map keeps part of the information unchanged, so any copy that gets
   in must share that part with the real voice. The polynomial mixes non-
   linearly, so copies that get in can be very different from the real voice.

Q: How do we know the results were not tuned to look good?
A: All choices were made on separate people and locked before the test data were
   opened, following a plan written in advance.

Q: What is still unknown?
A: Why a minority of polynomial keys cancel poorly, and whether the face results
   hold on a larger, separate face collection.
