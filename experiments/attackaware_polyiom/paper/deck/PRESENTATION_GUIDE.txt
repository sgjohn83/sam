Presentation Guide: AttackAware PolyIoM
A slide-by-slide guide to the 24 slides, with the actual numbers explained

THE STORY IN ONE MINUTE
=======================

A face or voice system does not keep your photo or recording. It turns it into a
list of numbers called an embedding (512 numbers for a face, 192 for a voice)
and stores that. If a hacker steals this list, they hold a copy of your face or
voice that you can never change.

The fix is to store a locked version of the list, made with a secret key. If it
is stolen, the company issues a new key, makes a new locked version, and the
stolen one should stop working. This is like cancelling a stolen bank card and
getting a new one with a new number.

Our system locks the data in two steps:
- Step 1, the polynomial: a secret formula mixes small groups of 5 neighbouring
  numbers together and shrinks the voice list from 192 numbers to 48.
- Step 2, IoM hashing: a second secret key creates many random combinations of
  those 48 numbers, sorts them into groups of 32, and in each group keeps only
  the position of the biggest one (a number from 1 to 32). For voice there are
  128 groups, so the stored template is just 128 small numbers, for example 7,
  31, 2, 19 and so on.

Our three main findings:
- Step 1 is what makes cancelling work. Without it, a stolen template kept
  working after the key was changed in all 80 out of 80 tests.
- The polynomial hides the real voice: an attacker who steals the template can
  rebuild only a rough, mostly wrong copy of the original voice numbers.
- That hiding costs some accuracy: about 1.2 extra mistakes per 100 checks on
  voice. So there is a trade-off.

WORDS USED IN THE SLIDES
========================

- Embedding: the list of numbers a model makes from a face photo or a voice
  clip.
- Template: the locked version of the embedding that the system stores.
- Genuine attempt: a person trying to log in as themselves. Impostor attempt:
  someone trying to log in as another person.
- EER (equal error rate): the error rate when the system is set so that it
  wrongly rejects real users exactly as often as it wrongly accepts impostors.
  1.93% means about 2 mistakes in every 100 attempts. Lower is better.
- TAR (true accept rate): out of 100 genuine attempts, how many are accepted.
  Higher is better.
- FMR (false match rate): out of all impostor attempts, how many are wrongly
  accepted. 0.1% means 1 in 1,000. Lower is better.
- pp (percentage points): the plain gap between two percentages. From 0.273% to
  1.928% is a gap of 1.655 pp.
- Dsys (linkability): if someone has two locked templates made with different
  keys, can they tell whether both belong to the same person? 0 means they
  cannot tell at all; 1 means they always can. Lower is better.
- Cosine (similarity score): how close the attacker's rebuilt copy is to the
  real embedding. 1 means identical, 0 means unrelated. For comparison, a random
  guess scores 0.120 on voice.
- Interval, for example 1.93% [1.09, 2.92]: the true value is very likely
  somewhere between 1.09% and 2.92%. We get it by reshuffling the people 2,000
  times and recalculating.
- Firm: we say a difference is firm when its whole interval is on one side of
  zero, so it cannot be explained by luck.

PART 1: THE QUESTION
====================

Slide 1: Title
--------------

ON THE SLIDE: The title "Keyed compression is what makes an IoM-based cancelable
biometric revocable", the line "A pre-registered evaluation on face and voice",
and a diagram showing how a template is built.

WHAT IT MEANS: "Keyed compression" is Step 1: shrinking the data with a secret
key. "Revocable" means a stolen template can be cancelled. "Pre-registered"
means we wrote down our test plan and our pass marks before we looked at the
test data, so we could not adjust the plan afterwards to make the results look
better.

SAY: This talk is about keeping stored face and voice data safe, and about which
part of the protection lets us cancel a stolen copy.

Slide 2: Abstract
-----------------

ON THE SLIDE: Four cards numbered 01 to 04.

WHAT IT MEANS:
- Card 01 describes the system: Step 1 (polynomial) then Step 2 (IoM hashing).
- Card 02: on 58 speakers the system had never seen, the voice error rate is
  1.93%, about 2 mistakes per 100 attempts. On 110 speakers from a completely
  different recording collection it is 2.80%. Linkability is 0.088 and 0.080,
  close to the ideal value of 0.
- Card 03: protection makes voice accuracy worse by 1.84 pp on the new speakers
  and by 2.79 pp on the different collection. An attacker who knows every secret
  rebuilds a voice copy with similarity 0.222. Without the polynomial the copy
  reaches 0.913, which is almost identical to the real voice.
- Card 04: if Step 1 is removed, a stolen template keeps working after a key
  change in all 80 tests.

SAY: The system recognises people well, keeps templates unlinkable, hides the
real voice, and can be cancelled. Each of these has a measured cost, and we
report it.

Slide 3: The problem
--------------------

ON THE SLIDE: "Passwords can be changed. Biometrics cannot." and four numbered
requirements from the international standard ISO/IEC 24745.

WHAT IT MEANS: A locked template must pass four tests:
- 01 Recognition: it must still recognise the right person nearly as well as an
  unlocked system.
- 02 Irreversibility: a thief must not be able to turn the template back into
  the face or voice.
- 03 Unlinkability: two templates of the same person made with different keys,
  for example by a bank and by a hospital, must not be matchable to each other.
- 04 Revocability: after a theft, a new key must make the stolen template
  useless.
Tests 01 to 03 can be done on one template at one moment. Test 04 needs a story
in two parts: steal a template, change the key, then try the stolen template
again. That is why most studies skip it. We do it.

SAY: Most studies check three of the four requirements. We also check the
fourth: does cancelling actually work?

Slide 4: The system and our three questions
-------------------------------------------

ON THE SLIDE: Four coloured boxes in a row (Embedding, Keyed polynomial, IoM-GRP
under key R, Template) and three numbered questions with answers.

WHAT IT MEANS:
- Question 01: if we use only Step 2 (IoM hashing) on the raw voice numbers, can
  a stolen template be cancelled? No. An attacker can rebuild the real voice
  numbers almost exactly (similarity 0.913). Those numbers are the person's real
  voice, and the real voice does not change when the key changes. So the rebuilt
  copy is accepted under every new key: 80 out of 80 tests.
- Question 02: what fixes this? Adding Step 1. Now the attacker can only rebuild
  the shrunk, key-mixed version, which is tied to the old key. When the key
  changes, that copy no longer fits. A simple key-based shrinking (a linear map)
  works too.
- Question 03: so what does the polynomial add, compared with the simple linear
  map? Better hiding: the attacker's copy scores 0.222 with the polynomial and
  0.490 with the linear map. The cost is 1.23 pp more voice errors.

SAY: Cancelling works because of the secret shrinking step. The polynomial adds
extra hiding of the real voice, and that hiding has a price.

Slide 5: Contributions and summary of findings
----------------------------------------------

ON THE SLIDE: Six numbered contributions on the left, and four big numbers on
the right: 1.93%, 0.088, 0.222 and 80/80.

WHAT IT MEANS: The six contributions are the extra tests we added:
- We removed one step at a time to see what each step does. This is called an
  ablation.
- We ran the same strong attack on every version.
- We tested whether a stolen template stops working after a key change.
- We compared every result with the same system without any protection.
- We explained why each step behaves as it does.
- We followed a test plan fixed in advance.
The four numbers are the headline results: 1.93% voice errors, 0.088
linkability, 0.222 attacker similarity, and 80 out of 80 failures to cancel
without Step 1.

SAY: Our contribution is not only a system. It is a stricter way of testing
systems like it.

Slide 6: Background
-------------------

ON THE SLIDE: Three cards (2.1, 2.2, 2.3) and a box called "The gap" (2.4).

WHAT IT MEANS:
- 2.1: Older methods such as BioHashing lock data with a random projection. If
  the key is stolen as well, the projection can be undone and the real data
  recovered.
- 2.2: IoM hashing stores only "which one was biggest" in each group, never the
  values themselves, so a lot of information is thrown away on purpose.
- 2.3: Many studies have three weak spots. They choose the pass mark using the
  same people they report results on. They test the full system but never each
  step on its own. They say "the data cannot be recovered" without actually
  trying to recover it.
- 2.4: Our study avoids all three, and adds the cancelling test.

SAY: We test the things other studies usually take for granted.

PART 2: HOW WE DID IT
=====================

Slide 7: The face and voice models
----------------------------------

ON THE SLIDE: Two coloured columns, Face (blue) and Voice (green), each with
four rows: architecture, weights, front end, output.

WHAT IT MEANS:
- Face: the FaceNet model, trained by others on a large face collection
  (VGGFace2). A face detector (MTCNN) first finds and crops the face to 160 by
  160 pixels. Photos with no detectable face are left out. Output: 512 numbers.
- Voice: the ECAPA-TDNN model from the SpeechBrain toolkit, trained by others on
  VoxCeleb. Audio is used at 16,000 samples per second. Output: 192 numbers.
- "Frozen" means we used both models exactly as downloaded, with no retraining.
  So if results get better or worse, it is because of our protection, not
  because we changed the model.

SAY: We used standard, ready-made models without changes, so the results show
the effect of our protection only.

Slide 8: How a template is made and checked
-------------------------------------------

ON THE SLIDE: Two diagrams. The top one shows a template being built; the bottom
one shows two templates being compared.

WHAT IT MEANS, for voice:
- 3.3 Hardening (Step 1): the secret polynomial takes 5 neighbouring numbers at
  a time, mixes them into 1, then slides along. 192 numbers become 48.
- 3.4 IoM-GRP (Step 2): a second key makes 128 groups of 32 random combinations
  of the 48 numbers. In each group only the position of the largest is kept. The
  template is 128 positions, each between 1 and 32.
- 3.5 Matching: when the person logs in again, a new template is made the same
  way and the two are compared group by group. The score is the number of groups
  where the positions agree. For voice, if 37 or more of the 128 agree, the
  person is accepted. For face the pass mark is 68 out of 256.

SAY: The stored template is just a list of positions. Logging in means counting
how many positions match, and passing if enough do.

Slide 9: Choosing the key and the settings
------------------------------------------

ON THE SLIDE: Two cards, Stage A and Stage B, and a warning note at the bottom.

WHAT IT MEANS:
- Stage A, choosing the secret polynomial key: we generated 10,000 to 20,000
  candidate keys. Each was tried on a separate group of 50 people. A quick
  attack test (5 tries of 2,000 steps each) checked how hard each key was to
  reverse. Keys that harmed recognition were thrown out. The best remaining key
  was locked in and named K*.
- Stage B, choosing the settings: we tried 80 combinations of group count, group
  size and window overlap on a different group of 42 people. A combination
  passed if it had at most 1% errors and accepted at least 95% of genuine
  attempts. Among those that passed, we chose the one with the lowest
  linkability. The pass mark (37 for voice) was fixed at the same time.
- The warning note: the quick attack test in Stage A was only used to pick a
  key. It does not prove the final system resists attacks. That proof is on
  Slide 17.

SAY: Every choice was made on its own group of people and locked before the test
data were opened.

Slide 10: Splitting the people into groups
------------------------------------------

ON THE SLIDE: A diagram and four large numbers: 50, 42, 58 and 110.

WHAT IT MEANS: The people were split once, by a computer with a fixed random
seed (2026), into separate groups that never overlap:
- 50 people: used only to choose the key (Stage A).
- 42 people: used only to choose the settings (Stage B).
- 58 people: the main test. Their data was opened only after the key, settings
  and pass mark were locked.
- 110 speakers from a different recording collection (VCTK): opened last, as a
  final outside check.
"Sealing" means the key, settings and pass mark were saved in a locked record,
and every later analysis checks that it did not change them.

SAY: No one in the test groups was seen while we were building the system. This
is what makes the results trustworthy.

Slide 11: How we measured
-------------------------

ON THE SLIDE: Six measures (EER, TAR and FMR, Dsys, SAR, PRAR, Firm) and a box
explaining the intervals.

WHAT IT MEANS:
- EER, TAR, FMR and Dsys: see the word list at the start.
- SAR (attack success rate): out of all stolen templates, how many did the
  attacker turn into a fake input that the system accepted?
- PRAR (post-revocation acceptance rate): after the key is changed, how many of
  the attacker's fake inputs are still accepted? 0% means cancelling worked
  perfectly. 100% means it did not work at all.
- Intervals: we reshuffle the people 2,000 times and recalculate each result.
  The middle 95% of the results gives the interval. For the cancelling test we
  also reshuffle the keys, because some keys behave very differently from
  others.

SAY: We call a difference real only when the reshuffling shows it cannot be
explained by luck.

Slide 12: Tools and data
------------------------

ON THE SLIDE: Two tables: software with version numbers, and datasets with how
much of each was used.

WHAT IT MEANS:
- Software: PyTorch 2.11.0 for the calculations, facenet-pytorch 2.6.0 for
  faces, SpeechBrain 1.1.1 for voices, and NumPy, pandas and SciPy for the
  statistics.
- Faces: the LFW collection. Of its 13,233 photos, 7,581 had a detectable face;
  we kept people with at least 3 photos, which gave 900 people, and removed 100
  who also appear in another face collection.
- Voices: LibriSpeech, 150 speakers with 15 recordings each.
- Outside check: VCTK, 110 speakers and 1,650 recordings.
- The models ran on a normal processor and everything after that on one graphics
  card (Tesla T4), with every random choice fixed in advance.

SAY: Every tool and every dataset is recorded with its exact version, so anyone
can repeat the work and get the same numbers.

PART 3: RESULTS
===============

Slide 13: The locked settings
-----------------------------

ON THE SLIDE: A table of the locked settings for voice and face, a chart showing
how they were chosen, and a note that the face pass mark was relaxed.

WHAT IT MEANS:
- Voice: 128 groups of 32, pass mark 37. On the 42 development people this gave
  0.952% errors and accepted 95.2% of genuine attempts. 30 of the 80 settings
  met the target.
- Face: none of the 80 settings met the voice target. The best reached 2.29%
  errors and 90.08% acceptance. The reason: the face model on its own, with no
  protection at all, already makes 3.475% errors on these photos. So the face
  target was relaxed to at most 3% errors and at least 85% acceptance. 13
  settings then passed, and the chosen one uses 256 groups of 32 with pass mark
  68.
- This change was decided and written down before any test person's data was
  opened.

SAY: The face target was relaxed openly and before testing, because the face
model alone could not meet the stricter target.

Slide 14: Testing on new people
-------------------------------

ON THE SLIDE: A results table, a bar chart comparing protected and unprotected
error rates, and three labelled findings (firm, firm, not resolvable).

WHAT IT MEANS:
- Voice on 58 new speakers: 1.93% errors (likely between 1.09% and 2.92%). It
  accepts 94.0% of genuine attempts, and wrongly accepts 0.185% of impostor
  attempts, about 1 in 540.
- Face on new people: 4.80% errors, with a wide range (1.95% to 10.35%) because
  there are few photos per person.
- The bar chart compares the same people with and without protection.
  Unprotected voice makes almost no mistakes: 0.085%. So protection costs 1.84
  pp on the new speakers and 2.79 pp on the outside collection. Both are firm,
  because the protected range starts above anything the unprotected system could
  plausibly reach.
- For face, protection costs 1.32 pp, but the ranges overlap too much to be
  sure. That is what "not resolvable" means.
- We do not say "X times worse", because unprotected voice makes less than one
  mistake in the whole test. Dividing by a number that close to zero would give
  a misleading result.

SAY: On voice, protection costs about 2 extra mistakes per 100 attempts, and we
are sure of it. On face, we cannot yet tell how big the cost is.

Slide 15: Testing on a different voice collection
-------------------------------------------------

ON THE SLIDE: Four large numbers (2.80%, 89.00%, 0.080, 0.377%) and a chart
comparing the main test with the outside test.

WHAT IT MEANS:
- We took the voice system exactly as locked and used it, unchanged, on VCTK.
  This is a different collection with different speakers, accents and recording
  equipment. There were 1,100 genuine comparisons and 119,900 impostor
  comparisons.
- Errors: 2.80% (likely 1.99% to 3.81%). This is a little higher than 1.93% on
  the main test, but the ranges overlap, so there is no clear evidence of a real
  drop.
- Genuine attempts accepted: 89.0%. Linkability: 0.080, even lower than in the
  main test.
- One target was missed. The system wrongly accepted 0.377% of impostor
  attempts, about 1 in 265, while the goal was 0.1%, or 1 in 1,000. As planned,
  the pass mark set on the first collection was kept unchanged here.

SAY: On a completely new collection the system still worked well. We report
openly the one target it missed.

Slide 16: What the polynomial costs
-----------------------------------

ON THE SLIDE: Three versions of the system, and a stacked bar chart that splits
the voice error into parts.

WHAT IT MEANS: We built three versions and tested them on the same people:
- PolyIoM, our full system with the polynomial and the hashing: 1.928% errors.
- Linear map, where a simple key-based shrinking from 192 to 48 numbers replaces
  the polynomial: 0.700% errors.
- IoM only, hashing with no shrinking at all: 0.273% errors.
The chart stacks the differences:
- 0.273% comes from the hashing itself.
- 0.427 pp more comes from shrinking 192 numbers to 48.
- 1.228 pp more comes from the polynomial's mixing. This part is firm: its range
  is 0.29 to 2.19 pp, all above zero.
Linkability is similar for all three versions. For face, the data is not enough
to separate the versions.

SAY: Most of the accuracy cost comes from the polynomial. The next slide shows
what we get in return.

Slide 17: What an attacker can and cannot do
--------------------------------------------

ON THE SLIDE: A box describing the attacker, a large "100%", and a bar chart of
similarity scores.

WHAT IT MEANS:
- The attacker is the strongest possible one: they have stolen the template and
  also know both secret keys and how the system works. Starting from random
  numbers, they adjust a fake embedding step by step (800 steps, 5 fresh starts)
  until it produces the stolen template.
- Finding 1, the 100%: the attacker succeeded every time, for every version, on
  both face and voice. Each fake was accepted by the system. This is because the
  system only compares templates, so any input that makes the right template
  gets in.
- Finding 2, the chart: how close is the fake to the person's real voice?
  - IoM only: 0.913. The fake is almost the real voice.
  - Linear map: 0.490. About halfway.
  - PolyIoM: 0.222. Close to a random guess (0.120).
  - For face the scores are 0.876, 0.492 and 0.418.
- In plain terms: the attacker can open the door, but with the polynomial they
  do not learn what the person's voice really is.

SAY: A thief who knows every secret can fool the system. But with the
polynomial, they learn very little about the real person's voice. That is
exactly why cancelling must work.

Slide 18: Can a stolen template be cancelled?
---------------------------------------------

ON THE SLIDE: A bar chart of PRAR, three large numbers (80/80, 12/80, 0/80), a
dot chart with one dot per key, and a box headed "A correction we made".

WHAT IT MEANS: The test runs like this. The attacker builds a fake from a stolen
template. The company changes the keys and re-enrols everyone. Then the attacker
tries the old fake again. We repeated this with 40 new keys for voice and 40 for
face, 80 in total.
- IoM only: the old fake still worked for every person, under every new key:
  100%, 80 out of 80. Cancelling does not work, because the fake is basically
  the real voice.
- PolyIoM: on average, 7.5% of old fakes still worked for voice and 25.6% for
  face. For most keys, the number was 0%. But for 12 of the 80 keys, half or
  more of the fakes still worked, and for 8 of those, all of them did. We do not
  yet know why these keys behave badly.
- Linear map: steady and low, 5.7% for voice and 2.9% for face. No key let half
  or more through.
- The correction box: an earlier test with only 3 keys suggested that PolyIoM
  was clearly worse than the linear map, by 4.6 pp. When we repeated it with 40
  keys and reshuffled the keys too, the difference became -1.8 pp, with a range
  from -8.8 to +4.0, which crosses zero. So the earlier claim was wrong, and we
  withdrew it.

SAY: Without the secret shrinking step, cancelling never works. With it,
cancelling works for most keys. We also show one earlier claim that did not
survive a bigger test.

Slide 19: Summary of results
----------------------------

ON THE SLIDE: One table with the four requirements, what the system achieved,
and a label for each: "confirmatory" or "secondary".

WHAT IT MEANS:
- Recognition: 1.93% errors on new speakers and 2.80% on a new collection.
  Confirmatory.
- Accuracy cost: 1.84 pp and 2.79 pp on voice, both firm; face unclear.
  Confirmatory.
- Linkability: 0.088 and 0.080, near the ideal 0. Confirmatory.
- Hiding the real voice: 0.222 against 0.913 without the polynomial, but the
  attacker still gets in 100% of the time. Secondary.
- Cancelling: works for most keys, and only because of the secret shrinking
  step. Secondary.
"Confirmatory" means the test was in the plan written in advance and was run
once. "Secondary" means we added it afterwards, so it is supporting evidence.

SAY: All four requirements in one table, each marked with how strong the
evidence is.

PART 4: WHAT IT MEANS
=====================

Slide 20: Discussion
--------------------

ON THE SLIDE: Three cards: 6.1, 6.2 and 6.3.

WHAT IT MEANS:
- 6.1: The polynomial hides the real voice (0.222 against 0.913) but does not
  stop a fake from being accepted. A study that measured only "how well can the
  voice be rebuilt" would conclude the system is safer than it really is.
- 6.2: On recognition, the three versions differ by about 1 to 2 pp. On
  linkability they hardly differ. On the attack, they all fail equally. Only the
  cancelling test separates them clearly: 100% versus 7.5% versus 5.7%. So
  cancelling is the most useful test, yet most studies never run it.
- 6.3: Advice for other researchers: attack the simple versions with the same
  attack, test each step separately, and test many keys, not just a few.

SAY: The test that best tells designs apart is the one most studies skip.

Slide 21: Which design to choose
--------------------------------

ON THE SLIDE: Two columns, the keyed polynomial on the left and the keyed linear
map on the right, joined by a bar labelled TRADE.

WHAT IT MEANS:
- Accuracy: polynomial 1.928% errors, linear map 0.700%. The linear map is
  better by 1.23 pp, and this is firm.
- Hiding the real voice: polynomial 0.222, linear map 0.490. The polynomial
  hides much more. For face: 0.418 against 0.492.
- Cancelling: with the polynomial, 12 of 80 keys let half or more of the fakes
  through. With the linear map, 0 of 80.
- When to choose the polynomial: when the worst outcome is someone learning a
  person's real face or voice, which can never be changed.
- When to choose the linear map: when the worst outcome is a stolen login, which
  a new key can fix.
- Either way: always include the secret shrinking step. Without it, a stolen
  template works forever.

SAY: Neither design wins on everything. Choose the polynomial to protect the
biometric itself, and the linear map for higher accuracy and more reliable
cancelling.

Slide 22: Limitations
---------------------

ON THE SLIDE: Ten numbered limits, one line each.

WHAT IT MEANS:
- 01: only 58 test people, so face results describe what happened but cannot
  prove much.
- 02: only voice was checked on a second collection; face was not.
- 03: only one type of attack was tried; a cleverer attack might do better.
- 04: the 40 new keys were made from the same family as K*, not chosen through
  Stage A again.
- 05: we do not know why 12 of the 80 polynomial keys failed to cancel well.
- 06: the linear map used the settings tuned for the polynomial, not settings of
  its own.
- 07: face enrols with one photo, while voice uses the average of five
  recordings.
- 08: unprotected voice made almost no mistakes, so we give limits rather than
  ratios.
- 09: the face model alone makes about 3% errors, which caps how good face can
  be.
- 10: the list used to remove overlapping people came from an unofficial copy
  and matched exact names only.

SAY: These limits tell the audience how far to trust each result. None of them
changes the main findings.

Slide 23: Conclusion
--------------------

ON THE SLIDE: The main message on a dark background, three large numbers, a
recommendation, and a line about checking the results.

WHAT IT MEANS:
- The main message: the secret shrinking step makes cancelling possible, and the
  polynomial hides the real biometric. Choosing between the polynomial and the
  simpler linear map is a trade-off.
- The three numbers: 1.93% voice errors on new speakers; 80 of 80 failures to
  cancel without the shrinking step; and 0.222 against 0.490, how much of the
  real voice an attacker recovers with the polynomial versus the linear map.
- The recommendation for researchers: attack the simple versions too, test each
  step, compare with the unprotected system, and test what happens after a key
  change.
- The last line: anyone can type one command (python3 run.py verify). It runs 74
  checks and 29 tests, and confirms that two separate programs produce exactly
  the same 32,768 template values.

SAY: Shrink the data with a secret key so templates can be cancelled. Add the
polynomial if hiding the real biometric is worth a small loss of accuracy. And
every number in this talk can be checked with one command.

Slide 24: Declarations
----------------------

ON THE SLIDE: Three short statements: AI use, data and code, and ethics.

WHAT IT MEANS:
- AI use: an AI assistant helped write the code for the extra analyses, the

- Data and code: the code, the stored results, the 29 tests and the checking

  anyone else's data.

SAY: We state clearly how AI was used, what we share and why, and why the attack
experiments are safe.

THREE THINGS TO REMEMBER
========================

- The secret shrinking step is what makes cancelling work. Without it, a stolen
  template kept working in 80 out of 80 tests.
- The polynomial hides the real voice: an attacker's copy scores 0.222, close to
  a random guess, against 0.490 with a simple linear map.
- That hiding costs about 1.2 extra voice errors per 100 attempts, and
  cancelling works less evenly across keys. It is a trade-off, not a free gain.

QUESTIONS PEOPLE MAY ASK
========================

Q: The attacker gets in 100% of the time. Isn't the system broken?
A: That attacker already knows every secret key, which is the worst case. It is
   exactly why cancelling matters: once a theft is discovered, the keys are
   changed, and our tests show that the old fakes then stop working for most
   keys.

Q: Why not just use the linear map, since it is more accurate?
A: Because it reveals more of the real voice (0.490 against 0.222). A stolen
   password can be changed, but a stolen face or voice cannot.

Q: How do we know you did not tune the results to look good?
A: The key, the settings and the pass mark were chosen on separate people and
   locked before the test people were opened. The outside collection was tested
   last, without any changes.

Q: Can someone else repeat this?
A: Yes. Every software version and random seed is recorded, and one command re-
   checks every number from the stored results.

Q: What is still unknown?
A: Why 12 of the 80 polynomial keys cancel poorly, and whether the face results
   hold on a larger, separate face collection.
